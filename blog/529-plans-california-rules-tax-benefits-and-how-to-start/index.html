529 Plans California: Everything You Need to Know Before You Open One

College costs are rising fast. At many California public universities, a single year of tuition, housing, and fees can run $30,000 or more. For parents with young kids, that number can feel overwhelming. But here's the thing: starting early with a 529 plan can make a massive difference. If you save just $200 a month starting when your child is born, you could have a meaningful college fund built up by the time they turn 18.

529 plans California families use most often come with specific rules, tax benefits, and investment options worth understanding before you open an account. This guide walks you through how these accounts work, what California does (and doesn't) offer, and how to get started in plain English.

What Is a 529 Plan and How Does It Work?

A 529 plan is a tax-advantaged savings account designed specifically for education expenses. The name comes from Section 529 of the Internal Revenue Code. Every U.S. State sponsors at least one, but you're not locked into your own state's plan. A California resident can open a 529 plan from Nevada, New York, Utah, or anywhere else.

Here's how the account actually works. You contribute after-tax dollars. The money grows inside the account free from federal income taxes. And when you withdraw the money to pay for qualified education expenses, those withdrawals are also federal income tax-free. That combination of tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals is what makes 529s so powerful over time.

What Counts as a Qualified Expense?

This matters a lot because pulling money out for the wrong reason triggers taxes and a 10% penalty. Qualified expenses generally include:

  • Tuition and mandatory fees at colleges, universities, and trade schools
  • Room and board (if the student is enrolled at least half-time)
  • Books, supplies, and required equipment
  • Computer hardware and software used primarily for school
  • K-12 tuition up to $10,000 per year per beneficiary (per IRS rules)
  • Apprenticeship programs registered with the U.S. Department of Labor
  • Student loan repayment up to $10,000 lifetime per beneficiary

Non-qualified withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income on the earnings portion, plus that 10% penalty. So the key is planning your withdrawals carefully to match actual school costs.

Does California Offer a State Tax Deduction for 529 Plans?

This is the most important California-specific fact, and it surprises a lot of people. California does not offer a state income tax deduction for 529 plan contributions. Many states do. Virginia, New York, and Illinois all let residents deduct contributions from state taxable income. California does not.

The reason this matters is that California has one of the highest state income tax rates in the country, with a top rate above 13%. So the absence of a state deduction is a real difference compared to residents of other states who can reduce their state tax bill by contributing to a 529. California families are effectively missing out on a benefit that residents in roughly 35 other states receive.

But that doesn't mean 529 plans California families use aren't worth it. The federal tax benefits are still fully available to California residents. And since California taxes investment gains, using a 529 for tax-free growth at the state level is actually still beneficial because California conforms to the federal 529 rules on tax-free earnings. You just can't deduct the contribution going in.

California's Own 529 Plan: ScholarShare 529

California's official 529 plan is called ScholarShare 529, administered by the ScholarShare Investment Board. It's a well-regarded plan with some genuinely competitive features. Let's break down what it offers.

Investment Options Inside ScholarShare 529

ScholarShare 529 offers a solid lineup of investment choices. These include:

  • Age-based portfolios: These automatically shift to more conservative investments as your child gets closer to college age. A common approach is to start aggressive when the child is young (mostly stocks) and gradually shift toward bonds and stable value funds as the college years approach.
  • Static portfolios: You pick an allocation and it stays fixed. This suits people who want direct control over their investment mix.
  • Individual fund options: Including index funds from TIAA and other providers with generally low expense ratios.

Expense ratios matter because fees compound over time just like returns do. Even a difference of 0.50% per year in fees on a $50,000 account adds up to thousands of dollars over 18 years. Checking expense ratios before picking a portfolio is worth the 10 minutes it takes.

Contribution Limits and Account Rules

California's ScholarShare 529 follows federal contribution guidelines. There's no annual contribution limit set by the IRS specifically for 529s, but there is a total account balance limit. California's limit is $529,000 per beneficiary across all 529 accounts held for that child. Once the account balance hits that ceiling, you can't make additional contributions, though the account can still grow past that point through investment returns.

For gift tax purposes, the IRS annual exclusion in 2026 is $18,000 per year per donor. You can contribute up to that amount per year without triggering gift tax reporting. There's also a strategy called superfunding (sometimes called 5-year election or front-loading), where you contribute up to $90,000 in a single year and elect to spread it over five years for gift tax purposes. This can be a smart approach for grandparents who want to make a large one-time contribution to a grandchild's education fund.

Should California Residents Use ScholarShare or Another State's Plan?

Since California doesn't offer a state tax deduction anyway, there's no financial penalty for using another state's plan. That opens up the field. Some of the plans California residents commonly compare include:

  • Utah's my529: Consistently rated among the best 529 plans nationally for its flexibility, low fees, and investment options.
  • Nevada's Vanguard 529: Known for low-cost index fund investing with no state income tax issues for Nevada residents, and open to anyone.
  • New York's 529 Direct Plan: Also well-regarded for low fees, though the state deduction only benefits New York residents.

The honest answer is that ScholarShare 529 is competitive. Its expense ratios are reasonable, the age-based options are solid, and it's easy to open online. But if you prefer Vanguard index funds specifically, Nevada's plan might appeal to you. Since the state tax deduction question is off the table for Californians, the comparison comes down to investment options, fees, and ease of use.

How Much Should You Actually Save in a 529 Plan?

This depends on a few variables: how old your child is now, what school they might attend, and if you're planning to cover 100% of costs or just help. There's no single right answer, but thinking through some numbers can help frame the decision.

Suppose you open a 529 when your child is born with an initial deposit of $1,000, then contribute $250 per month. Assuming a 6% average annual return over 18 years, you'd accumulate roughly $95,000 to $100,000. That's a meaningful amount. It might cover two years of in-state tuition at the University of California, or about one year at a private university.

If your child is already 10 years old when you start, you have about eight years to save. The same $250 per month at 6% gets you closer to $30,000. That's still valuable, but it's a clear illustration of why starting early matters so much. The math behind compounding rewards patience because returns stack on top of returns over time.

How to Open a 529 Plan in California: Step by Step

Opening a ScholarShare 529 account is genuinely straightforward. Here's a general walkthrough of the process.

Step 1: Gather Your Information

You'll need your Social Security number, your child's Social Security number, and basic contact information. You'll also want your bank account details ready for the initial deposit and ongoing contributions.

Step 2: Go to the ScholarShare 529 Website

California's plan is managed at the official ScholarShare website. You can open an account entirely online in about 15 minutes. There's no minimum contribution required to open the account, which is helpful for families who want to start small.

Step 3: Choose Your Investment Portfolio

If you're not sure which portfolio to pick, the age-based option is the most common starting point. You select the portfolio matched to your child's birth year, and the plan adjusts the investment mix automatically as they get older. This removes the need to actively manage the account over time.

Step 4: Set Up Automatic Contributions

This step matters because consistency is what actually builds the account. Setting up a monthly automatic transfer from your checking account means the savings happen without requiring a decision each month. Even $50 per month adds up significantly over 18 years.

Step 5: Tell Family Members They Can Contribute

Grandparents, aunts, uncles, and family friends can contribute to a 529 plan. ScholarShare and most other plans have a gifting feature or can accept third-party contributions. Birthdays and holidays become opportunities to grow the fund instead of adding toys to a pile.

What Happens If Your Child Doesn't Go to College?

This is a common worry. The good news is that 529 plans are more flexible than they used to be. Here are your options if college isn't in the picture:

  • Change the beneficiary: You can transfer the account to another family member, including siblings, cousins, or even yourself, without penalty. The definition of family member is fairly broad under IRS rules.
  • Use it for trade school or apprenticeships: Many vocational programs qualify as eligible institutions.
  • Roll over to a Roth IRA: Starting in 2024, a rule change allows 529 account balances to be rolled into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary, subject to certain limits and a 15-year account holding requirement. This is a significant new flexibility option worth researching further.
  • Non-qualified withdrawal: You can take the money out, but you'll owe income tax on earnings plus the 10% penalty. This is the least favorable option, but it's always available.

The 529-to-Roth IRA rollover rule is relatively new, so it's worth verifying current IRS guidance directly at IRS.gov before making any decisions based on it.

People Often Ask

Does California have its own 529 plan?

Yes. California's official 529 plan is called ScholarShare 529, administered by the ScholarShare Investment Board. It's open to all U.S. Residents, not just Californians, and has no minimum contribution to open an account.

Is there a California state tax deduction for 529 contributions?

No. California does not offer a state income tax deduction for 529 plan contributions. This is different from most other states. You still get the federal tax benefits, including tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals for qualified expenses, but there's no California-specific deduction going in.

Can a California resident open a 529 plan from another state?

Absolutely. Since California doesn't offer a state tax deduction, there's no financial penalty for choosing another state's plan. Californians commonly look at Utah's my529 and Nevada's Vanguard 529 as alternatives, both of which are open to any U.S. Resident.

What is the maximum contribution to a 529 plan in California?

California's ScholarShare 529 has a total account balance limit of $529,000 per beneficiary across all 529 accounts held for that child. There's no annual contribution cap, but annual contributions above $18,000 per donor (the 2026 gift tax annual exclusion) may require gift tax reporting with the IRS.

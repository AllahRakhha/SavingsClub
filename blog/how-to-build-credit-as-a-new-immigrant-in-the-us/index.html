Starting From Zero: How to Build Credit When You're New to the US

You just arrived in the United States. You have savings, a job lined up, and a plan. But when you try to rent an apartment, buy a car, or even get a cell phone contract, you hit the same wall every time: "Sorry, we can't find a credit history for you." It's frustrating because it feels like a catch-22. You need credit to get credit. But nobody explains how to actually start. This guide explains exactly how to build credit from scratch as a new immigrant, step by step, with real dollar examples so you can see what's actually at stake.

Why Your Credit Score Matters More Than You Might Think

A credit score is a three-digit number, typically ranging from 300 to 850, that tells lenders how reliably you pay back borrowed money. In the US, the most widely used scoring model is the FICO Score, developed by the Fair Isaac Corporation. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau notes that this score influences everything from loan approvals to the interest rates you're offered.

Here's why this matters in dollar terms. Suppose you want to borrow $25,000 for a car. With no credit history, you might get a loan at 14% APR. Someone with a strong credit score might get the same loan at 6% APR. Over a 60-month loan, that difference costs you roughly $5,500 more in interest. That's not a small number. That's real money leaving your pocket every single month just because your score is thin or nonexistent.

And it's not just loans. Landlords in states like California, Texas, and New York routinely pull credit reports before renting apartments. Some employers check credit during hiring. Even utility companies in states like Florida and Illinois may require a security deposit of $200 or more if you have no credit file. The gap between "no credit" and "good credit" touches almost every part of your financial life in America.

What Counts as "No Credit" vs. "Bad Credit" (and Why the Difference Is Important)

Many new immigrants assume they have bad credit because they were rejected for a card or loan. That's usually not the case. No credit (also called a "thin file") and bad credit are two very different situations, and they require different strategies.

  • No credit / thin file: You have little or no US credit history. The bureaus simply don't have enough data to generate a score yet. This is the most common situation for new immigrants.
  • Bad credit: You have a US credit history, but it includes missed payments, collections, or high balances. This takes longer to fix.

The good news for most new immigrants is that you're starting fresh. A thin file is fixable relatively quickly. Many financial educators suggest that with consistent, responsible credit use, you can go from no score to a score above 700 in 12 to 24 months. That's not guaranteed, but it's a realistic target that many people working the basics achieve.

Your foreign credit history, unfortunately, doesn't transfer automatically. The US credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion) maintain separate systems from other countries. Some programs are starting to change this, but as of 2026, most new immigrants genuinely do start at zero in the US system.

How to Build Credit: The Core Strategies That Actually Work

Strategy 1: Get a Secured Credit Card

A secured credit card is the most common starting point for people with no credit history. Here's how it works: you deposit money (usually $200 to $500) with a bank, and that deposit becomes your credit limit. You use the card like a normal credit card, and the bank reports your payment activity to the credit bureaus each month.

This works because payment history is the single biggest factor in your FICO score, making up 35% of the total calculation. Every on-time payment adds a positive mark. Every missed payment adds a negative one. So getting a secured card and paying it off in full every month is the fastest way to establish a track record.

Some practical tips on secured cards:

  • Look for cards with no annual fee or a low annual fee (under $35).
  • Make sure the bank reports to all three major credit bureaus, not just one. Ask before you apply.
  • Keep your balance below 30% of your credit limit each month. If your limit is $300, don't carry more than $90 in charges at any time. This affects your credit utilization ratio, which makes up 30% of your FICO score.
  • Many secured cards convert to unsecured cards after 12 to 18 months of on-time payments, returning your deposit.

Strategy 2: Become an Authorized User on Someone's Account

If you have a family member or close friend in the US with good credit, ask them to add you as an authorized user on their credit card. You don't even need to use the card. The account's history (including its age and payment record) can appear on your credit report, giving you a head start.

This works because the credit bureaus treat authorized user accounts as part of your credit file. A card that's been open for five years with no missed payments can meaningfully raise your score, even if you personally never charged a dollar to it. The account owner takes on no real risk as long as they don't give you physical access to the card.

Strategy 3: Apply for a Credit-Builder Loan

A credit-builder loan works differently from a regular loan. Instead of getting money upfront, you make monthly payments into a locked savings account. When the loan term ends (usually 12 to 24 months), you receive the total amount you paid in. The lender reports each payment to the credit bureaus the entire time.

Many community banks and credit unions in states like Georgia, Illinois, and Ohio offer credit-builder loans. A typical structure might be a $1,000 loan paid over 12 months at around $85 to $90 per month. At the end, you get roughly $1,000 back (minus a small interest fee). You built credit AND saved money at the same time. That's a genuinely useful tool.

Strategy 4: Use Experian Boost or Similar Programs

Experian offers a free program called Experian Boost that lets you add on-time payments for utilities, phone bills, and even streaming services to your Experian credit file. If you've been paying your phone bill on time since you arrived, those payments could count immediately.

This won't work with all lenders (since it only affects your Experian file), but it can help you get a score faster than waiting for a credit card to age. It's worth exploring as a supplementary step, not a replacement for a secured card.

How Much Does Each Factor Actually Affect Your Score?

Understanding what moves your credit score is important because it helps you focus your energy in the right places. Here's how the FICO model breaks it down:

  • Payment history (35%): On-time payments are the most powerful factor. One missed payment can drop a good score by 60 to 110 points.
  • Credit utilization (30%): How much of your available credit you're using. Lower is better. Aim to stay under 30%, ideally under 10%.
  • Length of credit history (15%): Older accounts help your score. This is why starting sooner matters.
  • Credit mix (10%): Having both revolving credit (cards) and installment loans (car loan, credit-builder loan) helps.
  • New credit inquiries (10%): Applying for too many accounts at once can temporarily lower your score since each hard inquiry signals potential risk to lenders.

The takeaway: focus almost entirely on payment history and utilization in your first year. The other factors build naturally over time.

How Fast Can You Really Build Credit From Scratch?

This is probably the question most new immigrants have, and the honest answer is: it depends, but here's a realistic timeline.

  • Month 1 to 3: Open a secured card or credit-builder loan. You may not have a score yet because bureaus typically need 3 to 6 months of account history to generate one.
  • Month 4 to 6: Your first FICO score may appear. It could be anywhere from 580 to 650 depending on your utilization and payment record.
  • Month 7 to 12: With consistent on-time payments and low utilization, scores in the 650 to 700 range become realistic.
  • Month 13 to 24: Scores above 700 become achievable, especially if you've added a second credit account and kept utilization low.

Suppose you open a secured card in January 2026 with a $300 limit. You charge $60 per month (20% utilization) and pay it off in full every month. By January 2027, you could realistically have a score in the 680 to 720 range. That's a score that qualifies you for most standard credit cards, some car loans, and many apartment rentals without a co-signer.

Common Mistakes That Slow Down Your Credit Building

Some mistakes set people back significantly. Here are the ones worth knowing upfront:

  • Paying only the minimum: This keeps your utilization high and costs interest. Pay the full balance every month.
  • Applying for too many cards at once: Each application triggers a hard inquiry. Apply for one card at a time, and wait at least six months before applying for another.
  • Closing your first card too soon: Closing an old account reduces your available credit and shortens your average account age. Keep your first secured card open even after you upgrade to a regular card.
  • Missing a payment by even one day: Some lenders report late payments after 30 days. But even a payment a few days late can trigger a late fee and hurt your relationship with the issuer. Set up autopay for at least the minimum payment as a safety net.
  • Not checking your credit report: Errors on credit reports are more common than most people realize. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau allows you to dispute errors for free. Check your reports at AnnualCreditReport.com regularly.

What About Banking First? A Note on the Foundation

Before you can open a secured card, you typically need a US bank account. Most major banks will open a checking or savings account for you with a foreign passport and Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN) even if you don't yet have a Social Security Number. An ITIN is issued by the IRS and can be used in place of an SSN for many banking and tax purposes.

Some community banks and credit unions are specifically welcoming to immigrants and don't require a Social Security Number to open an account. Getting banked first makes every other step easier because it establishes a relationship with a financial institution that may later offer you a secured card or credit-builder loan.

People Often Ask

How long does it take to build credit from scratch in the US?

Most people get their first credit score within 3 to 6 months of opening their first account. Getting to a score above 700 typically takes 12 to 24 months of consistent on-time payments and low credit utilization. Starting sooner matters because the length of your credit history is a factor in your score.

Can I build credit in the US without a Social Security Number?

Yes. Many banks and credit unions accept an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN) instead of a Social Security Number to open accounts and apply for secured credit cards. Some lenders also accept foreign passports and visa documentation. Requirements vary by institution, so it's worth calling ahead before applying.

What credit score do I need to rent an apartment?

Most landlords in major US cities look for a score of 620 or higher, though requirements vary by city and property. In competitive markets like New York or San Francisco, some landlords prefer scores above 700. If your score is below 620, a larger security deposit or a co-signer can sometimes substitute.

Does my credit history from another country transfer to the US?

In most cases, no. The US credit bureaus operate independently from credit reporting systems in other countries. As of 2026, some newer programs are attempting to bridge this gap for immigrants from select countries, but the standard experience is starting with no US credit file. Building from scratch is the realistic plan for most new arrivals.

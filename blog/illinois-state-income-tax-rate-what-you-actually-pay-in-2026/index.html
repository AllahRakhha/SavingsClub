Illinois State Income Tax Rate: What You Actually Pay in 2026

Most people know their gross salary. But ask them what they actually take home after Illinois taxes, and things get fuzzy fast. If you live or work in Illinois, the Illinois state income tax rate is one of the most important numbers affecting your paycheck every two weeks. The good news is that it's simpler than you might think. Our SavingsClub Research Team built our interactive Illinois Income Tax calculator above to help everyday Americans break down complex state income tax numbers without confusing bank jargon. Let's walk through exactly what Illinois taxes, how much it costs you, and what you can do about it in 2026.

How the Illinois State Income Tax Rate Actually Works

Illinois has a flat income tax. That means every taxpayer pays the same percentage of their income, no matter how much they earn. In 2026, that rate is 4.95%.

This is different from how federal income taxes work. The federal system uses progressive tax brackets, where higher income gets taxed at higher rates. Illinois doesn't do that. A person earning $30,000 pays the same 4.95% rate as someone earning $300,000. The flat structure makes Illinois relatively easy to calculate, which is genuinely useful for budgeting.

Here's why the flat rate matters for your planning: because there are no brackets to jump across, a raise or side income in Illinois won't suddenly push part of your income into a higher state tax tier. Every extra dollar you earn in the state gets taxed at exactly 4.95%, and that predictability makes budgeting a lot cleaner.

What Income Does Illinois Tax?

Illinois taxes most types of income, including:

  • Wages and salaries from employment
  • Self-employment income
  • Rental income
  • Lottery and gambling winnings
  • Most retirement distributions (with some exceptions)

One important exception: Illinois does not tax Social Security benefits. It also exempts most pension income for government retirees and military pension income. If you're planning retirement in Illinois, that's a significant detail worth understanding. Real Dollar Examples: What 4.95% Looks Like on Your Paycheck

Percentages are easy to say. Dollars are easier to feel. So let's put real numbers to this.

Imagine you earn $50,000 a year working in Chicago. Your Illinois state income tax bill for the year would be roughly $2,475 (that's $50,000 times 4.95%). Spread across 26 biweekly paychecks, that's about $95 per paycheck going to the state.

Now suppose your household earns $90,000 a year. The math stays simple: $90,000 times 4.95% equals $4,455 per year, or about $171 per biweekly paycheck.

At $120,000 a year, the Illinois income tax bite comes to $5,940 annually.

These numbers don't include federal income tax, FICA taxes (Social Security and Medicare), or any local taxes. But seeing the state piece in isolation helps you understand exactly what you owe Illinois specifically, and why your gross pay and your actual deposit look so different from each other.

Does Illinois Have a Standard Deduction or Personal Exemption?

Illinois doesn't use a standard deduction the way the federal government does. But it does offer a personal exemption, which reduces the amount of income subject to state tax.

For 2026, the Illinois personal exemption amounts are:

  • Single filer: $2,425
  • Married filing jointly: $4,850
  • Per dependent child: $2,425 per qualifying dependent

So if you're a single filer earning $50,000, you'd subtract $2,425 first. That leaves $47,575 of taxable income. At 4.95%, your actual Illinois tax bill comes to roughly $2,355, not $2,475. That's about $120 less than the straight calculation. Small, but real money.

A family of four filing jointly with two dependent children could subtract as much as $9,700 from taxable income before the 4.95% rate even applies. On a $70,000 income, that brings taxable income down to $60,300 and saves the household roughly $480 compared to paying the flat rate on the full amount.

The reason these exemptions exist is to provide some relief for households with lower incomes or dependents. Illinois designed them as a partial offset to the flat tax, since a flat rate by definition takes a larger percentage of a lower earner's spendable income in practical terms.

Is the Illinois Income Tax Rate High Compared to Other States?

Here's a fair question people ask a lot: how does Illinois stack up against neighboring states?

Let's compare the flat or effective state income tax rates of some Midwest neighbors in 2026:

  • Illinois: 4.95% flat
  • Indiana: 3.05% flat
  • Iowa: moving toward a 3.8% flat rate
  • Wisconsin: progressive brackets up to 7.65%
  • Michigan: 4.25% flat
  • Missouri: progressive, top rate around 4.8%

Illinois sits in the middle of the pack for the Midwest. Indiana and Michigan are lower. Wisconsin's top bracket is notably higher. States like Florida and Texas have no state income tax at all, which is why some higher earners relocate there.

But here's the thing: comparing state income tax rates in isolation doesn't tell the full story. Illinois also has relatively high property taxes and a sales tax rate that varies by county and city. In Chicago, the combined sales tax can reach over 10%. So your total state and local tax burden in Illinois is likely higher than the 4.95% income tax rate alone suggests.

How Does the Illinois Income Tax Affect Your Take-Home Pay?

When you add everything together, the gap between gross pay and take-home pay in Illinois can surprise people. Let's walk through a realistic breakdown for someone earning $60,000 a year in 2026.

Approximate annual deductions from a $60,000 Illinois salary:

  • Federal income tax (standard deduction applies): roughly $5,000 to $6,500 depending on filing status
  • Social Security tax (6.2%): $3,720
  • Medicare tax (1.45%): $870
  • Illinois state income tax (4.95% after personal exemption): roughly $2,845

That's a combined total of roughly $12,435 to $13,935 in taxes before any retirement contributions or health insurance premiums are removed. Your take-home pay ends up somewhere around $46,000 to $47,500 per year, or about $1,769 to $1,827 every two weeks.

The reason knowing this matters is simple: budgeting from your gross income will wreck your plan. You can't spend money you don't actually receive. Building any savings goal, from an emergency fund to a down payment, has to start from your real after-tax number.

What About Local Income Taxes in Illinois?

Illinois cities, including Chicago, do not levy a local income tax. That's actually a meaningful benefit compared to states where cities pile on extra local tax. New York City residents pay both New York State income tax and a New York City income tax on top of that. Philadelphia has its own wage tax. But in Illinois, once you've handled the state flat rate, you don't owe an additional city income tax.

That said, Chicago and other municipalities do impose other taxes, including local sales taxes, real estate transfer taxes, and various business taxes. So the overall cost of living in a major Illinois city isn't low by any stretch. But at least your paycheck isn't getting dinged with a local income tax on top of the 4.95% state rate.

Illinois Tax Credits That Can Reduce What You Owe

Beyond the personal exemption, Illinois offers a handful of tax credits that can directly reduce your final tax bill. A credit is different from a deduction because it reduces what you owe dollar for dollar, not just what income gets taxed.

Some credits worth knowing about:

  • Illinois Earned Income Credit: Worth 20% of your federal Earned Income Tax Credit amount. This is specifically designed to help lower-income working families and can reduce or eliminate your Illinois tax bill entirely.
  • Property Tax Credit: If you own your home in Illinois, you may be able to claim a credit equal to 5% of the Illinois property taxes you paid. Since Illinois property taxes are among the highest in the country, this credit can add up.
  • Education Expense Credit: Parents sending children to private or non-public schools can claim a credit of up to $750 per family for qualified education expenses.
  • Investing in Kids Credit: This targets scholarships for low-income students and provides a 75% credit for qualifying donations to scholarship-granting organizations.

The reason Illinois created these credits is to offer targeted relief for working families and homeowners within the constraints of a flat tax system. They're worth checking each year when you file, since many taxpayers don't claim credits they're entitled to.

How to Plan Around the Illinois Flat Tax Rate

Since Illinois taxes income at a fixed 4.95%, your planning approach is different than it would be in a progressive-bracket state. Here are some practical strategies worth considering.

Strategy 1: Maximize Pre-Tax Retirement Contributions

Contributing to a traditional 401(k) or traditional IRA reduces your federal taxable income. Illinois, however, doesn't allow a deduction for IRA contributions on the state return. But your 401(k) contributions do reduce Illinois taxable wages as reported by your employer. So putting $10,000 into your 401(k) for the year reduces your Illinois taxable income by $10,000, saving you about $495 in state taxes alone. Use our 401(k) Calculator to see how contributions compound over time.

Strategy 2: Use a 529 Plan for Education Savings

Illinois offers a deduction of up to $10,000 per taxpayer ($20,000 for married couples filing jointly) for contributions to the Bright Start or Bright Directions 529 college savings plans. At 4.95%, a $10,000 contribution saves a single filer about $495 per year in Illinois taxes. That's a genuine, straightforward incentive to save for education inside the state plan.

Strategy 3: Track Property Tax Payments

Because the property tax credit equals 5% of your Illinois property taxes paid, keeping careful records of what you pay matters. If your annual property tax bill is $8,000, that credit is worth $400 directly off your Illinois tax bill. Given how high Illinois property taxes run in counties like Cook, Lake, and DuPage, this credit can be one of the more valuable ones available.

People Often Ask

What is the Illinois state income tax rate in 2026?

Illinois uses a flat income tax rate of 4.95% for all individual taxpayers in 2026. This rate applies to wages, salaries, self-employment income, and most other taxable income. It hasn't changed from recent prior years, and there's no bracket system that increases the rate at higher income levels.

Does Illinois tax Social Security income?

No. Illinois does not tax Social Security retirement benefits. It also generally exempts most pension income from government employment and military service. This makes Illinois notably more retirement-friendly than states like Colorado or Minnesota, which do tax Social Security to some extent.

How much Illinois income tax will I owe on $75,000?

On $75,000, a single filer would subtract the $2,425 personal exemption first, leaving $72,575 of taxable income. At 4.95%, that's roughly $3,592 in Illinois state income tax for the year. This doesn't include federal taxes, FICA, or any credits you might qualify for.

Does Chicago have a separate city income tax?

No. Chicago does not charge a separate city income tax on wages. Illinois residents only pay the 4.95% flat state income tax, not an additional municipal tax. This is different from cities like New York or Philadelphia, which levy their own local income or wage taxes on top of state income tax.
